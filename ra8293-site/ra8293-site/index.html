About RA 8293

RA 8293, also known as the Intellectual Property Code of the Philippines, is the main law protecting intellectual creations. It also created the Intellectual Property Office of the Philippines (IPOPHL). It has been amended, notably by RA 10372 (2013), which updated copyright provisions.

Part I – IPO

Creation and functions of the Intellectual Property Office.

Part II – Patents

Inventions: new, inventive, industrially applicable. Term: 20 years.

Part III – Trademarks

Marks, service marks, trade names, and unfair competition.

Part IV – Copyright

Literary and artistic works, including computer programs. Term: generally lifetime of the author + 50 years.

Intellectual Property Rights

IP refers to creations of the mind. The Code recognizes several categories relevant to IT students:

Copyright

Protects the expression of ideas (code, text, images, music). Arises automatically upon creation.

Patent

Protects technical inventions. Registration required.

Trademark

Protects brand names, logos, and slogans.

Industrial Design

Protects the ornamental look of a product or interface item.

Trade Secret

Confidential business information kept secret through reasonable measures.

Moral rights (attribution and integrity) and economic rights (reproduce, distribute, rent, adapt) are the two kinds of rights held by a copyright owner.

Source Code Ownership

Source code is a written work, so the creator is the initial owner. Ownership changes depending on the situation (see Sec. 178 on copyright ownership):

SituationWho generally owns it
Personal project, own time and resourcesThe programmer
Employee, as part of regular dutiesThe employer, unless a contract says otherwise
Commissioned (freelance) workThe creator keeps copyright unless a written agreement says otherwise
Student project in schoolDepends on school policy and any instructor or sponsor agreement
Group projectCo-authors, unless agreed otherwise

Tip: always put ownership terms in writing before starting a project.

Copyright and Software

Under the Code, computer programs are protected as literary works (Sec. 172.1). A computer program is a set of instructions that causes a computer to perform a task or achieve a result.

Protected

  • Source code and object code
  • Documentation and manuals
  • Original UI design and graphics
  • Database structure (as compilations)

Not protected (Sec. 175)

  • Ideas, concepts, and procedures
  • Methods and systems as such
  • Algorithms as abstract ideas
  • Mere facts and data

Copyright protection is automatic; registration or a notice (©) is not required but helps as evidence. Patents generally do not cover "programs for computers" as such (Sec. 22), which is why copyright is the main protection for software. The owner may make a single back-up copy of a program they lawfully hold (Sec. 189).

AI-Generated Code & IP

The Code was written before generative AI, so it has no express provision for AI output. Copyright is traditionally tied to a human author, which raises open questions:

  • Authorship: Fully AI-generated code with no human creativity may not qualify for copyright. Human selection, modification, and integration can strengthen a claim.
  • Training data: Models may be trained on copyrighted or open-source code, which raises infringement and license questions.
  • Licensing risk: AI output can resemble licensed code (e.g., GPL), creating obligations you may not notice.
  • Accountability: The developer who ships the code is responsible for it.

Rules differ across countries and are still evolving. Check current IPOPHL guidance and your tool's terms of service.

AI Usage Agreement

Organizations and schools often require an agreement governing AI tools. A good one covers:

Disclosure

State when and how AI was used in the work.

Confidentiality

Never paste trade secrets, credentials, or client data into public AI tools.

Review

Humans must test, review, and understand AI code before use.

Ownership

Who owns outputs, and what the AI provider's terms allow.

Licensing

Check generated code for third-party or open-source license conflicts.

Software as Trade Secret

Software can be protected as a trade secret, such as unreleased source code, algorithms, and system architecture. Information qualifies when it is secret, has commercial value because it is secret, and the owner takes reasonable steps to keep it secret. Misappropriation may be actionable under unfair competition rules and contract law.

  • Use non-disclosure agreements (NDAs).
  • Restrict repository and server access.
  • Mark code and documents confidential.

Unlike copyright, a trade secret is lost once it becomes public, and independent discovery is not infringement.

Fair Use & Legal Limitations

Sec. 185 allows limited use of copyrighted work for purposes such as criticism, comment, news reporting, teaching, scholarship, and research. Courts weigh four factors:

  1. Purpose and character of the use (commercial or non-profit educational)
  2. Nature of the copyrighted work
  3. Amount and substantiality of the portion used
  4. Effect on the potential market or value of the work

Other limitations (Sec. 184–190) include use for judicial proceedings, certain classroom uses, and the back-up copy of software. Copyright also expires, after which the work enters the public domain.

Intellectual Property Agreements

Assignment

Transfers ownership of the IP to another party, in writing.

License

Permits use while ownership stays with the owner (exclusive or non-exclusive).

NDA

Protects confidential information shared between parties.

Employment / Work-for-hire

Defines ownership of work made during employment.

Software License (EULA, Open Source)

Sets terms such as MIT, Apache, GPL, or proprietary licenses.

Under the Code, assignments and licenses should be in writing and signed to be enforceable, and some must be recorded to bind third parties.

Copyright vs. Plagiarism

Copyright InfringementPlagiarism
NatureLegal violationEthical / academic offense
ConcernUsing work without the owner's permissionPresenting someone's work as your own
ConsequenceCivil and criminal liabilityFailing grade, suspension, reputational harm
Can citing fix it?No, permission or a legal exception is neededYes, proper credit usually addresses it

Example: copying licensed code and crediting the author may avoid plagiarism but still infringe copyright. Using public domain code without credit is not infringement but may still be plagiarism.

Infringement Issues

Infringement occurs when someone exercises the owner's exclusive rights without authority. Common software examples:

  • Software piracy: unlicensed copying or cracked software
  • Copying code from a repository ignoring its license
  • Reselling or redistributing proprietary software
  • Removing copyright notices or license headers

Civil remedies

Injunction, damages, and impounding of infringing copies (Sec. 216).

Criminal penalties

Imprisonment and fines for willful infringement (Sec. 217).

Administrative

Complaints and mediation through IPOPHL.

Case Studies / Scenarios

Click each item to reveal the analysis.

Pearl & Dean held copyright over technical drawings of advertising light boxes, and the Supreme Court held that copyright over the drawings did not extend to the light boxes themselves, which would need a patent. Lesson: copyright protects expression, not the underlying useful article or idea.
The Court found that portions of a college textbook were copied from another author's book without proper authorization, and discussed how limited and properly attributed borrowing differs from substantial copying. Lesson: attribution does not excuse copying a substantial part.
Ana is a salaried developer who builds an inventory tool as part of her job. Her employer generally owns the copyright (Sec. 178), unless the contract states otherwise.
Ben prompts an AI to write his entire capstone and submits it without disclosure. This may breach school AI policy (academic dishonesty), raises licensing risk, and weakens any copyright claim as there is little human authorship.
A startup copies a GPL library into its proprietary app. This may violate the license terms and expose the company to infringement claims. The fix is to comply with the license, replace the library, or obtain a different license.

Scenarios are hypothetical, for learning only. Verify case details in the Supreme Court e-Library.
